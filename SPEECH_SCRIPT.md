## 01 Aitor | Introduction: why the alveolus needs careful protection
**Pointing cue.** Point briefly to the alveoli and the alveolar macrophage. Do not preview the intracellular steps.

Every time we breathe, air carries more than oxygen: some microorganisms and fine particles can reach the alveoli, where alveolar macrophages help defend the lung. [1]
But an inflammatory response can also injure the surface it is meant to protect. [1]
Alveolar macrophages are tissue-resident cells of the innate immune system, positioned on the air-facing side of the alveolar epithelium. [1] They are professional phagocytes, but engulfing material is only part of their role. [1,4]
They also help break down excess surfactant, the material that supports alveolar stability, and remove cells undergoing programmed cell death. [2,3] Clearance of these dying cells is called efferocytosis, and it can promote the resolution of inflammation rather than amplify it. [1,3]
So, the biological problem is not simply how to remove something from outside the cell. It is how to handle it while preserving the tissue around it. That is the question connecting our poster, from this bacterium on the left to the lung tissue below.
Nerea, let's begin at the cell surface: how does the macrophage recognise and engulf a bacterium?

## 02 Nerea | Phagocytosis of bacteria: from recognition to digestion
**Pointing cue.** Follow the bacterium from receptors and pseudopodia through the phagosome to the phagolysosome. Do not explain Rab and SNARE fusion machinery.

We begin with bacterial recognition. Phagocytes—such as macrophages and neutrophils—identify bacteria through two main pathways. Macrophages detect microbial components through pattern-recognition receptors, such as Toll-like receptors, while specialised phagocytic receptors mediate particle uptake. In opsonization, bacteria are coated by host proteins like IgG antibodies or complement fragments, allowing phagocytic receptors to bind them efficiently. [4–6]
Once bound, we move to engulfment and pseudopodia formation. Receptor binding triggers intracellular signaling cascades that reorganize the cell's actin cytoskeleton. The macrophage extends arm-like membrane projections called pseudopodia around the bacterium. These pseudopodia wrap around the pathogen until their membranes fuse, completely enclosing it. [4,7]
This leads to phagosome formation and maturation. The newly formed intracellular vesicle is the early phagosome, which has limited degradative capacity but may already have active antimicrobial mechanisms. It then undergoes interactions with endosomal compartments.
Regulated by Rab GTPases, the phagosome acquires proton pumps—V-ATPases—that progressively lower its internal pH. [4,8,9]
Next is phagolysosome formation. As the phagosome matures, it moves deeper into the cell and fuses with lysosomal compartments. This fusion creates a hybrid organelle—the phagolysosome— delivering digestive enzymes directly to the trapped pathogen. [4,8]
Finally, we have bacterial degradation. The acidic environment, often with a pH around 4.5 to 5, allows acid hydrolases—such as proteases, lipases, nucleases and lysozyme—to break down bacterial components. [8] Alongside this, NOX2 can generate reactive oxygen species, even before the mature phagolysosome has formed. These mechanisms destroy many engulfed bacteria, although some pathogens can resist them. [9]
But where did the digestive enzymes come from? Urtzi will follow their journey through the cell.

## 03 Urtzi | Lysosomal biogenesis: making and delivering the enzymes
**Pointing cue.** Follow one hydrolase from RER to cis-Golgi, TGN, endosome and lysosome. Point out that the M6P receptor follows a separate recycling route.

The enzymes have their own journey, running alongside phagocytosis rather than beginning only after a bacterium arrives. [10]
We will follow a typical soluble lysosomal hydrolase from synthesis to its destination.
A signal sequence directs the growing protein into the lumen of the rough endoplasmic reticulum during synthesis. [10]
There, the protein folds and receives N-linked carbohydrate chains; glycan processing also participates in protein quality control. [10] Correctly processed cargo leaves the ER in COPII-dependent carriers and moves towards the Golgi apparatus. [11]
The crucial sorting information is then added to its carbohydrate chain. [10]
At the cis-Golgi, GlcNAc-phosphotransferase transfers a phospho-GlcNAc group onto selected mannose residues. [10]
An uncovering enzyme removes the outer GlcNAc, exposing mannose-six-phosphate, or M6P. [12]
This is the targeting label, not an enzyme and not a tag added directly to the protein backbone. [10,12]
At the trans-Golgi network, M6P receptors bind the tagged hydrolases on the luminal side of the membrane. [10]
Their cytosolic tails interact with sorting adaptors, helping package the cargo into carriers associated with clathrin. [13]
This links recognition inside the compartment to transport machinery outside it. [13]
The carriers deliver their cargo to endosomes. [10,14]
The acidic environment favours dissociation of the hydrolase from its receptor; the receptor can then return to the Golgi for reuse. [14]
What separates here is the enzyme from the receptor, not simply a phosphate falling off the enzyme. [14]
Notice that the enzyme is soluble cargo, whereas the receptor remains part of a membrane. [14]
The return arrow therefore tracks receptor recycling, not the delivery of digestive enzymes back to the ER. [14]
Hydrolases enter the dynamic endolysosomal system, where an acidic lumen and appropriate enzyme processing support degradation. [10]
We have followed a major route for soluble lysosomal enzymes, not every pathway that builds a lysosome. [10]
We now have a bacterium-containing compartment and the machinery needed to digest its contents. But being in the same cell is not enough. Eneritz will explain how the correct membranes meet and fuse.

## 04 Eneritz | Integration: Rab GTPases, SNAREs and recycling
**Pointing cue.** Begin where the late phagosome meets the lysosome. Use the fusion inset, then the arrows showing the fate of degradation products.

Inside the macrophage, phagosomes, endosomes and lysosomes must locate and fuse with one another accurately, without random mixing. This precision involves two major regulatory protein families: Rab GTPases and SNARE proteins. [15–17]
First, Rab GTPases act as molecular switches. They alternate between an inactive GDP-bound state and an active GTP-bound state. Rab5 is associated with early phagosomes, while Rab7 contributes to later maturation and interactions with lysosomal compartments. [8,15]
Rab proteins possess a lipid anchor acquired through prenylation. GEFs promote exchange of GDP for GTP, activating Rab; active Rabs recruit effectors, including motor-associated and tethering proteins. GAPs then stimulate GTP hydrolysis, and GDP-bound Rab can be recycled with the help of GDI. [15]
These effectors bring the correct membranes close together, but docking is not yet fusion. The HOPS tethering complex illustrated on our poster is one example of machinery involved in these endolysosomal encounters. [15,16]
For the final step, complementary SNARE proteins—traditionally called v-SNAREs on the vesicle and t-SNAREs on the target—assemble across the two opposing membranes into a four-alpha-helix trans-SNARE complex. Their progressive zippering pulls the membranes together and drives fusion.
The resulting cis-SNARE complex is then disassembled by NSF and alpha-SNAP so the proteins can be reused. [16,17]
As Nerea explained, acid hydrolases and antimicrobial mechanisms break down the engulfed bacterium. But what happens to the resulting molecular fragments? [4]
Some breakdown products, such as amino acids and sugars, can be transported across the membrane into the cytosol for reuse. [10] Other antigenic peptides can be loaded onto MHC class II molecules and displayed on the macrophage surface, where antigen-specific CD4-positive T cells may recognise them. [18]
We have seen how this machinery helps the macrophage handle bacteria. Nora will now explain why persistent mineral particles can lead to a very different outcome.

## 05 Nora | Pathology: silicosis and asbestosis
**Pointing cue.** Begin at the silica particles and the long asbestos fibre. End by pointing to the fibrotic alveolar tissue, not the phagolysosome again.

Both silicosis and asbestosis are classic examples of pneumoconioses: occupational lung diseases caused by inhalation and retention of mineral particles. Silicosis is associated with respirable crystalline silica, especially during mining and stone cutting; asbestosis is caused by inhaled asbestos fibres, historically encountered in insulation and construction. [19,21]
Although both can lead to chronic inflammation and pulmonary fibrosis, their mechanisms of macrophage injury differ in important ways. [19,21]
Let's start with silica. Alveolar macrophages can engulf silica particles, which reach phagolysosomal compartments but cannot be digested by lysosomal hydrolases. Some particles can damage lysosomal membranes, releasing contents such as cathepsins into the cytosol. With an appropriate priming signal, this injury can contribute to activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. [20]
NLRP3 promotes activation of caspase-1, which processes pro-IL-1 beta into the mature inflammatory cytokine IL-1 beta. Alongside mediators such as TNF-alpha and excessive reactive oxygen species, this can sustain inflammation and injure nearby cells. ROS can damage membrane lipids, proteins and DNA, while inflammatory mediators recruit additional immune cells. [20,21]
With asbestos, fibre length and biopersistence become especially important. A macrophage may extend pseudopodia along a long fibre but fail to close the phagocytic cup. This is called frustrated phagocytosis. The cell may then sustain inflammatory signalling and release reactive oxygen species or degradative components. Shorter fibres can be engulfed, although uptake does not necessarily guarantee clearance. Engulfed asbestos can also cause lysosomal injury; the two pathologies are not entirely separate mechanisms. [21]
Persistent injury and inflammation promote activation of fibroblasts and excessive collagen deposition. This is pulmonary fibrosis: the alveolar walls become thicker and stiffer, which makes breathing more difficult and impairs gas exchange. [22]
Aitor, this brings us back to our opening question: when does engulfment actually amount to successful defence?

## 06 Aitor | Conclusion: what counts as successful defence?
**Pointing cue.** Return to the whole-cell diagram, then briefly to the injured lung tissue. End facing the audience.

We began by asking how the lung can defend itself without damaging the surface it needs for breathing.
Vesicular trafficking provides organisation: it brings cargo and digestive machinery together in controlled compartments. [4,10,15] But organisation alone cannot make every material degradable or harmless. [19–21]
That is why uptake is not the same as successful defence. A macrophage may engulf a particle correctly, yet persistent injury and inflammatory signalling can still damage the surrounding lung. [19–22]
Our conclusion is that successful defence depends on both coordinated cellular machinery and the biological consequences of the material being handled. [1,19–22]
The aim is not simply to engulf. It is to preserve the tissue's ability to function—and, here, the lung's ability to breathe.
